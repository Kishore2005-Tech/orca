# PROBLEM_STATEMENT.md

# ORCA — Marine Ecosystems Reasoning with Collaborative Agents

**SIH Problem ID:** SIH26176
**Organization:** Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO)
**Category:** Software
**Domain:** Marine Ecosystems · Earth Observation · Geospatial Intelligence · Agentic AI · Decision Intelligence

---

## 1. Official Problem Statement

> **ORCA — Marine EcOsystem Reasoning with Collaborative Agents**

The official SIH26176 problem statement describes a need for an **Agentic AI-powered conversational platform** that enables users to access, analyse, and reason over marine information using natural language.

The problem context identifies that marine ecosystems are important to livelihoods, food security, biodiversity, maritime transportation, coastal resilience, and the blue economy. Large volumes of satellite Earth Observation and oceanographic information — including parameters such as **Sea Surface Temperature (SST), chlorophyll concentration, and weather information** — are generated by ISRO and other agencies.

Marine stakeholders need timely access to this information for operational planning and decision-making. The challenge is therefore to create a platform capable of:

* Understanding natural-language marine queries.
* Decomposing complex questions into executable tasks.
* Coordinating multiple specialised AI agents.
* Retrieving relevant marine, satellite, meteorological, and geospatial information.
* Performing spatial, temporal, and contextual reasoning.
* Correlating information from multiple heterogeneous sources.
* Producing explainable, context-aware recommendations.
* Presenting results through conversational interfaces, maps, alerts, and geospatial visualisations.
* Supporting contextual multi-turn interaction and Indian regional languages.

Representative questions in the official problem statement include:

* Where is the nearest Potential Fishing Zone today?
* Is it safe to venture into the sea tomorrow morning?
* What are the tide, weather, and sea conditions near my fishing location?
* Are there lightning or cyclone alerts in my area?
* Which regions show high chlorophyll concentration and favourable SST?
* What is the safest route for a fishing vessel considering weather and sea-state conditions?
* Why has fish productivity declined in a particular coastal region?
* Which fishing zones should be avoided because of hazardous conditions or geofencing restrictions?

The official requirement is therefore broader than a conventional marine-data visualisation system: it calls for **natural-language interaction, multi-source data integration, spatial-temporal reasoning, collaborative agents, and explainable decision support**.

# 2. Problem Statement Interpretation

## 2.1 What the problem is really asking

The central challenge is not simply:

> "How do we display marine data?"

It is:

> **How can heterogeneous marine information be converted into an understandable, context-aware, evidence-grounded answer to a user's actual question?**

A marine stakeholder may not think in terms of datasets, APIs, raster layers, variables, coordinate systems, forecast products, or metadata.

They think in terms of questions such as:

* "Can I go fishing tomorrow morning?"
* "Where should I fish today?"
* "What is happening near my location?"
* "Why has this region become less productive?"
* "Which areas should I avoid?"
* "What conditions are expected along my route?"

Answering these questions can require several information types simultaneously.

For example:

**"Is it safe to go fishing tomorrow morning?"**

may require consideration of:

`Location → Time → Weather → Wind → Waves → Sea state → Tides → Alerts → Restrictions → Relevant advisories`

The official problem therefore points toward a **reasoning and orchestration layer above existing information services**, rather than replacing those services.

### Classification

| Statement                                                                    | Classification                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Marine information comes from many observing and information systems         | **Verified fact**               |
| Marine variables have spatial and temporal dimensions                        | **Verified fact**               |
| Users may need multiple information types to answer one operational question | **Interpretation**              |
| ORCA should act as a reasoning/orchestration layer                           | **Proposed ORCA positioning**   |
| Collaborative agents can decompose and execute such tasks                    | **Proposed technical approach** |

# 3. Core Problem

The core problem can be expressed as:

> **Marine information exists in many forms, sources, variables, spatial scales, and temporal resolutions, while the questions posed by marine stakeholders are usually contextual and cross-domain. There is a gap between available information and an integrated, explainable answer to a specific user question.**

This is an **information-to-decision gap**.

It is important to distinguish this from a lack-of-data problem.

India already operates substantial ocean observation, forecasting, advisory, and information infrastructure.

For example, INCOIS states that it receives large volumes of oceanographic data in real time from **in-situ and remote-sensing observing systems**, covering physical, chemical, biological, and geological parameters across spatial and temporal domains.

Therefore:

> **The problem is not simply the absence of marine data. The problem is how efficiently and reliably users can discover, combine, interpret, and reason over the relevant information for a particular question.**

---

# 4. Why the Problem Exists

Marine intelligence is inherently complex because the ocean is:

* spatially extensive,
* dynamically changing,
* multidimensional,
* multidisciplinary,
* observed through different platforms,
* represented using different data products,
* and influenced by interacting physical, chemical, biological, and meteorological processes.

India's ocean-information infrastructure already reflects this complexity.

INCOIS manages ocean information derived from multiple observing systems, including satellites and in-situ platforms, while supporting ocean observations, ecosystem services, forecasting, climate services, and multi-hazard services.

ISRO's ocean-science programme similarly uses satellite observations to derive or support information concerning parameters and applications such as ocean colour, primary production, suspended sediments, ocean-surface winds, altimetry, potential fishing zones, sea-state forecasting, and coastal studies.

This creates a natural systems problem:

```text
Many observations
      ↓
Many datasets
      ↓
Many products/services
      ↓
Many spatial & temporal dimensions
      ↓
Many possible interpretations
      ↓
One user question
```

The difficulty lies in reliably moving from the bottom of this stack back to a useful answer.

# 5. Data Fragmentation

## 5.1 What fragmentation means

Marine information can originate from:

* Earth Observation satellites
* Oceanographic observing systems
* Buoys
* Argo platforms
* Weather services
* Ocean models
* Forecast systems
* GIS layers
* Marine advisories
* Coastal datasets
* Fisheries information
* Hazard-warning systems
* Regulatory/geofencing information

INCOIS explicitly describes its marine-data infrastructure as integrating information from both **in-situ observing systems and satellites**, with information spanning physical, chemical, biological, and geological parameters.

## 5.2 Why fragmentation matters

A user question rarely maps cleanly to one dataset.

For example:

```text
Question:
"Which fishing area is suitable tomorrow?"

Potential information:
        ├── SST
        ├── Chlorophyll
        ├── PFZ/advisory information
        ├── Wind
        ├── Waves
        ├── Weather forecast
        ├── Alerts
        └── Location / distance
```

The challenge is not merely retrieving each item.

The challenge is determining:

1. Which datasets are relevant?
2. Which observations correspond to the requested location?
3. Which time period matters?
4. Which data are observations versus forecasts?
5. How should conflicting information be handled?
6. How should the result be explained?

### Classification

**Verified fact:** Multiple marine observing systems and information services exist.

**Interpretation:** Cross-source questions create an information-integration burden.

**ORCA positioning:** Use agentic orchestration to identify, retrieve, correlate, and explain relevant information.

# 6. Spatial Heterogeneity

Marine conditions vary geographically.

A value measured or estimated at one location cannot automatically be treated as representative of another location.

Spatial variation can occur because of:

* coastal geometry,
* bathymetry,
* currents,
* fronts,
* river discharge,
* weather systems,
* biological productivity,
* seasonal ocean processes,
* distance from shore,
* and other oceanographic factors.

ISRO's ocean-science programme describes the use of satellite observations for studying ocean-surface and oceanographic parameters, while INCOIS provides information across spatial and temporal domains.

Therefore, a meaningful marine answer must preserve **where** an observation or prediction applies.

### ORCA implication

ORCA should treat location as a first-class reasoning parameter:

```text
User Location
      ↓
Spatial Query
      ↓
Relevant Marine Layers
      ↓
Spatial Filtering / Analysis
      ↓
Contextual Result
```

# 7. Temporal Heterogeneity

Marine information is also time-dependent.

Different information sources may represent:

* historical observations,
* near-real-time observations,
* forecasts,
* nowcasts,
* advisories,
* alerts,
* seasonal patterns,
* long-term trends.

INCOIS operates both near-real-time and forecast-based ocean-information services for fisheries, navigation, coastal management, disaster mitigation, and climate-related applications.

This means a question such as:

> "Is it safe tomorrow morning?"

cannot be answered appropriately using an undifferentiated collection of marine values.

The system must understand:

```text
WHERE?
WHEN?
OBSERVATION OR FORECAST?
VALIDITY PERIOD?
SOURCE?
```

### ORCA implication

ORCA should explicitly represent temporal context rather than treating all retrieved values as equally current.

# 9. Why Individual Datasets Are Insufficient

A single dataset generally describes only one aspect of the marine environment.

For example:

| Dataset / Variable | What it can tell us               | What it cannot establish alone |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| SST                | Thermal condition                 | Complete fishing suitability   |
| Chlorophyll        | Biological productivity indicator | Vessel safety                  |
| Wind               | Wind conditions                   | Biological productivity        |
| Wave height        | Sea-state information             | Fishing potential              |
| Tide               | Tidal state                       | Overall marine safety          |
| Weather forecast   | Atmospheric conditions            | Ecosystem productivity         |
| PFZ information    | Fishing-zone guidance             | Complete hazard picture        |
| Satellite image    | Spatial pattern                   | Complete operational context   |

Therefore:

> **The value of integrated marine intelligence comes from the relationship between relevant variables, not simply from displaying more variables.**

This is an interpretation of the technical problem, not a claim that individual official datasets are inadequate for their intended purposes.

Indeed, INCOIS already provides specialised services such as Potential Fishing Zone, tuna and hilsa fishery advisories, coral bleaching alerts, algal bloom information, jellyfish aggregation information, and multi-hazard services.

ORCA's role should therefore be **integration and reasoning across existing information**, not replacement of specialised products.

---

# 10. Why Conventional Dashboards Are Insufficient

A dashboard is valuable when users know:

* which parameter they need,
* which layer to open,
* what date/time to select,
* how to interpret the visualization,
* and how to combine multiple layers.

However, complex questions may require a sequence of analytical actions.

For example:

```text
User:
"Can I fish near Point X tomorrow at 6 AM?"

Traditional workflow:

Open weather
      ↓
Check wind
      ↓
Check wave forecast
      ↓
Check tide
      ↓
Check marine alerts
      ↓
Check fishing information
      ↓
Compare locations
      ↓
Interpret everything
      ↓
Make decision
```

The dashboard does not necessarily perform the complete reasoning process for the user.

### Important distinction

ORCA should **not** claim:

> "Dashboards are useless."

A more accurate statement is:

> **Conventional dashboards are primarily information-visualisation interfaces; ORCA proposes an additional conversational reasoning layer for cross-source questions that require retrieval, correlation, and contextual synthesis.**

This distinction is important because existing government marine information systems already provide valuable dashboards, apps, advisories, and data services. INCOIS, for example, provides the SAMUDRA application and multiple operational marine services.

# 16. Consequences of the Problem

The consequences should be stated conservatively.

The existence of fragmented or heterogeneous information does **not automatically imply that current systems fail**.

Rather, the potential consequences of an inefficient information-to-decision workflow include:

### Operational consequence

Additional effort may be required to obtain and combine relevant information.

### Decision-support consequence

Users may need to manually interpret several information products before forming a conclusion.

### Timeliness consequence

For time-sensitive questions, unnecessary information-processing steps can reduce efficiency.

### Accessibility consequence

Users without specialised knowledge may find complex datasets and technical products harder to use.

### Integration consequence

Useful relationships between variables may remain implicit rather than being surfaced automatically.

### Explainability consequence

A recommendation generated without visible evidence can be difficult to audit.

These are precisely the areas where an intelligent reasoning interface can potentially add value.

# 18. What ORCA Is NOT

Clear boundaries are essential.

## ORCA is NOT:

### 18.1 A replacement for official warning systems

ORCA should consume authoritative warnings and advisories rather than replacing them.

### 18.2 A replacement for INCOIS

INCOIS already operates major ocean-information, forecasting, ecosystem, and hazard services. ORCA should integrate with such services where technically and legally possible.

### 18.3 A replacement for ISRO Earth Observation infrastructure

ORCA is an application/decision-intelligence layer, not a satellite mission or Earth Observation processing infrastructure.

### 18.4 A new ocean observation network

ORCA does not need to deploy new satellites, buoys, Argo floats, or sensors.

### 18.5 A generic chatbot

Its defining capability should not be conversational fluency alone.

The system must connect conversation to:

```text
Intent
→ Data
→ Tools
→ Spatial reasoning
→ Temporal reasoning
→ Evidence
→ Explanation
```

### 18.6 An autonomous authority

ORCA should provide decision support, not claim authority over operational, regulatory, navigational, or safety decisions.

### 18.7 A universal predictor

ORCA should not claim that it can predict every marine phenomenon.

### 18.8 A black-box recommendation engine

Recommendations should be traceable to retrieved evidence and computational reasoning wherever possible.

# 21. A Concrete Example of the Problem

Consider the question:

> **"Is it safe to go fishing tomorrow morning near Chennai?"**

A simple chatbot could generate a plausible sentence.

That is **not sufficient**.

A credible ORCA workflow should instead determine:

```text
1. What does "tomorrow morning" mean?
2. What geographical area is intended?
3. What marine conditions are relevant?
4. Which official/authoritative sources are available?
5. What are the forecast conditions?
6. Are there relevant warnings or advisories?
7. Are there spatial restrictions?
8. Are there conflicts between information sources?
9. What evidence supports the answer?
10. What uncertainty remains?
```

The output should therefore resemble:

```text
QUESTION
   ↓
INTERPRET
   ↓
RETRIEVE
   ↓
CORRELATE
   ↓
ANALYSE
   ↓
EXPLAIN
   ↓
ANSWER
```

rather than:

```text
QUESTION
   ↓
LLM GUESS
   ↓
ANSWER
```

This distinction should be fundamental to the ORCA architecture.

# 27. Final Problem Definition

## Problem

Marine information is already being generated through satellite Earth Observation, ocean observing systems, numerical models, weather services, geospatial systems, and specialised advisory platforms. However, marine questions are often contextual, spatial, temporal, and multidisciplinary, requiring relevant information from several sources to be identified and interpreted together.

The challenge is therefore to build an intelligent interface that can bridge the gap between **heterogeneous marine information systems** and **human questions**.

## Required Capability

ORCA should enable a user to:

```text
ASK
 ↓
UNDERSTAND
 ↓
PLAN
 ↓
RETRIEVE
 ↓
CORRELATE
 ↓
REASON
 ↓
EXPLAIN
 ↓
DECIDE
```

with every important conclusion grounded in appropriate evidence.

## Proposed ORCA Position

> **ORCA is proposed as an Agentic Marine Intelligence and Decision-Support layer that orchestrates heterogeneous marine information sources, performs spatial-temporal and contextual reasoning, and converts complex marine questions into explainable, evidence-grounded conversational insights.**

The objective is not to create another isolated marine-data portal.

The objective is to make the **existing marine information ecosystem more queryable, interoperable, interpretable, and decision-oriented**.
